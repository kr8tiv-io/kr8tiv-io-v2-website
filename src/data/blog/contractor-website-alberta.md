# What an Alberta contractor's website needs to win the job in 2026

A contractor's website has one job: get a homeowner who has never heard of you to trust you enough to call. In Alberta, there is a shortcut to doing that well. The Province publishes a [checklist for homeowners hiring a contractor](https://www.alberta.ca/hiring-contractor). Build your site to answer that list, and you are answering the questions your best customers are already asking.

I built the brand and website for [Evolve Eco Blasting](/blog/evolve-eco-blasting-case-study/), an Alberta blasting company, and I look at a lot of trade sites. The pattern holds. The sites that bring in work are specific and full of proof. The ones that do not are a logo, a stock photo of a hard hat and a page called "Services".

Every government source below was checked on 19 November 2026.

## What Alberta tells homeowners to check

The Province's [hiring a contractor page](https://www.alberta.ca/hiring-contractor) tells homeowners to:

* Get written estimates from more than one business, with a full description of the work, the materials, start and finish dates, itemized costs, guarantees, and the payment method.
* Check that the tradespeople are qualified, with a Government of Alberta certificate where the trade requires one.
* Make sure the contractor has **Workers' Compensation Board coverage**, and ask for the WCB number.
* Ask to see an **insurance certificate** for public liability and property damage.
* Check whether permits or inspections are needed.
* Insist on a **written contract** and a clear payment schedule.

It also lists warning signs. Homeowners are told to avoid contractors who:

* Knock on doors with a "special price" because they are in the area.
* Quote a price without seeing the job.
* Demand a large down payment for materials.
* Refuse a written contract.
* Give only a **post office box** or an **answering service**.
* Will not commit to start and finish dates.

Read that second list again as a website owner. A site with no street address, a contact form and no phone number looks like the contractor the Province is warning people about.

## The prepaid contracting licence

This one catches a lot of renovation, landscaping and home-improvement businesses. Alberta requires a [prepaid contracting licence](https://www.alberta.ca/prepaid-contracting-licence) if you:

1. solicit, negotiate or sign contracts in person **anywhere other than your place of business**, such as the customer's home, **and**
2. take money before all the work is done.

It applies to construction, maintenance, repairs, alterations and improvements to private homes, including landscaping. Contracts for commercial buildings, contracts between contractors and subtrades, and some new-home warranty work are exempt.

To apply, you need a criminal record or police information check, a security, copies of your standard contracts, and the licence fee. Once licensed, the Province says you must be able to show proof on request, and that can include a link to the Province's Find a Licensed Business search, which is linked from the same page.

If you need the licence, put the licence number on your website and in your estimate template. Homeowners are told to check. Make it easy.

## The pages a contractor site needs

### A page for each service you want more of

Not one services page with eight bullet points. A page for basement development, a page for decks, a page for garage builds. Each one should say what the job involves, what affects the price, how long it usually takes, and show photos of that work.

This is also how search works. Someone typing "basement development Edmonton" is a better match for a page about basements than for a page about everything. That is the core of what we did for Evolve: separate pages for each kind of work, written for the people who buy it.

### A service area page that tells the truth

List the towns you actually work in: Edmonton, St. Albert, Sherwood Park, Spruce Grove, Leduc, Beaumont, Fort Saskatchewan, wherever it is. Say whether you charge for travel past a certain point.

Do not build a separate, near-identical page for every town with only the name swapped. Thin pages like that help nobody, and they make the site look like a template. One honest service-area page, plus real job stories that mention the neighbourhood, does more.

### Real project photos

Your own finished jobs, with a line about each: what the homeowner wanted, what you did, anything that was tricky. Stock photos of smiling builders are invisible. A photo of a deck you built in Terwillegar, with the before shot, is proof.

Get permission from the homeowner before you post photos of their home, and do not show house numbers.

### An "About" page with the boring facts

Who owns the business, how long you have been working, what tickets and certifications the crew holds, and the facts the Province tells homeowners to check:

* Your WCB account and how to request a clearance. WCB Alberta issues [clearance letters](https://www.wcb.ab.ca/insurance-and-premiums/clearance-letters/) that state whether a contractor has WCB coverage and whether the account is in good standing. Say on the site that you will provide one.
* Your liability insurance, and that you will provide the certificate.
* Your prepaid contracting licence number, if you need one.
* Your City of Edmonton business licence. See the [starting a business guide](/blog/starting-a-business-edmonton/) for how that works.

### A quote form that asks the right questions

The form should ask what you need to give a useful answer: the type of job, the town or neighbourhood, a rough size, the timeline, and an option to upload photos. Do not make every field required. Do say how fast you reply, and keep that promise.

If the form collects names, phone numbers and addresses, you need a privacy notice under Alberta's privacy law. [Alberta business website law](/blog/alberta-business-website-law/) covers what to put on the form.

### Prices, or at least a range

Homeowners want a number. "Most bathroom renovations we do land between X and Y" filters out tyre-kickers and saves you site visits. Pair it with what changes the price, and say clearly that a firm price comes after a site visit and a written estimate. That matches what Alberta tells homeowners to expect, so it builds trust instead of looking evasive.

### A contract and payment page

Explain your process: site visit, written estimate, written contract, deposit, schedule, payments, sign-off. If your deposit is small and tied to materials, say so. The Province warns homeowners about large down payments, so your policy is a selling point if it is reasonable.

## Outside the website

The website is half of it. The other half lives on Google:

* **Google Business Profile.** Most contractors should be set up as a service-area business with the home address hidden. Read [why a Business Profile does not show](/blog/google-business-profile-not-showing/) before you set it up.
* **Reviews.** Ask every customer at sign-off, the same way, with no incentive. [Here are the rules in Canada](/blog/google-reviews-canada-rules/).
* **Your truck and your signs.** A clean wrap with your phone number and web address is a moving billboard in every neighbourhood you work in.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a contractor website include in Alberta?

A page for each main service, the towns you actually serve, photos of your own finished jobs, a real business address and phone number, proof of WCB coverage and liability insurance, licence details where they apply, and a quote form that asks for the job details you need.

### Do Alberta contractors need a licence to take a deposit?

If you negotiate or sign contracts for work on a private home anywhere other than your place of business, such as the customer's kitchen table, and take money before the work is finished, Alberta requires a prepaid contracting licence. Commercial work and contracts between trades are exempt.

### Should a contractor put prices on their website?

Starting prices or typical ranges help, because they filter out people who cannot afford the job. Alberta also warns homeowners against contractors who quote a price without seeing the job, so make it clear that a firm price follows a site visit and a written estimate.
