# How to design packaging that stands out—and still works when it is printed

Build packaging around a clear product identity, one distinctive visual idea and a structure that protects and presents the contents. Use AI to explore directions, not to invent claims, barcodes or mandatory copy. Then test the design on the real dieline, in its sales context and as a finished physical proof.

Standing out does not mean covering every panel with an effect. The buyer still needs to know what the product is, which version they are holding and what they receive. Your printer needs files that can be produced. Your business needs packaging it can fill, ship and reorder without losing control of the artwork.

For an Edmonton brand selling across Canada, language and labelling decisions belong at the beginning. Expanding into North America also requires market-specific review; a Canadian label is not automatic approval for every country.

## Start with the product and route to market

Write a packaging brief before collecting visual references. Include the actual product dimensions, weight, material, handling needs and intended sales channels. A direct-to-consumer mailer, a shop-shelf carton and a label on a reusable container have different jobs.

Ask:

- What does the buyer need to recognise immediately?
- Which variants must be easy to distinguish?
- Will the package be handled, hung, stacked or shipped?
- What does the customer need before opening it?
- Which required information and claims need review?
- Who fills the package, and with what equipment?
- What can the business afford to hold in stock?

Define the market accurately. “Made for Edmonton customers” is not the same as “manufactured in Edmonton.” Do not introduce a local-origin claim merely because the design studio is local.

Collect real product specifications and approved copy. Keep provisional statements visibly marked inside the working brief so they cannot drift into production artwork as facts.

## Decide what the front needs to communicate

A useful front-panel hierarchy normally begins with identification, not decoration. The viewer needs to distinguish the brand, product and variant without deciphering a paragraph.

For a fictional home-fragrance range, the identity might remain consistent while the scent name and variant colour change. That is an illustration, not a client result or a claim about the regulatory category of a particular product.

Test three questions:

1. Can someone identify what the product is?
2. Can they distinguish it from the other variants?
3. Can they find the information needed to decide whether it suits them?

Do not make an abstract product name carry every explanation. A poetic name can sit alongside a plain-language description. Keep promotional claims subordinate to information the customer needs to understand the product.

The goal is a recognisable system. If each new flavour or size needs an unrelated redesign, the range will become harder to manage and compare.

## Use AI to explore a system, not one lucky image

Generate distinct directions from the same brief. Compare how each direction handles the whole range, the side panels, a small online thumbnail and the physical structure.

Adobe's [Text to Vector Graphic](https://www.adobe.com/products/illustrator/text-to-vector-graphic.html) can help explore editable vector artwork. Treat that as a starting point for illustration or patterns. Important typography, logos, technical marks and production geometry still need deliberate control.

A useful concept prompt might ask for three visual directions on the same supplied package structure, with clearly reserved areas for product identity, variant and reviewed label text. Tell the tool not to invent certifications, ingredient claims, country-of-origin statements or scannable codes.

Do not approve a design because the generated mockup looks realistic. Inspect whether the tool changed the package shape, omitted a panel or placed artwork across a closure. The attractive image may contain an idea worth keeping while being unusable as a production reference.

Use only source artwork and references you have permission to upload and use. Check the current terms of the exact feature and model rather than assuming every tool has the same commercial conditions.

## Establish the dieline before polishing the artwork

Ask the packaging supplier for the correct dieline and its file requirements. A dieline describes the flat structure and production features such as cuts, folds and panels. Its dimensions must match the package being ordered.

Keep the structural reference separate from print artwork according to the supplier's instructions. Confirm bleed, safe areas, glue areas, finishing layers and any white-ink or special-treatment requirements. Do not apply one universal bleed value to every supplier and package type.

Build a plain physical dummy when the structure warrants it. Put the real product inside. Check fit, closure, opening, assembly and how the design reads when the pack is folded.

Review panel orientation carefully. An elegant layout on a flat screen can put a side-panel message upside down after assembly. Check what becomes hidden behind a flap and whether essential information remains accessible in the sales configuration.

Where packaging touches food, cosmetics or another regulated product, ask the responsible supplier and compliance specialist to confirm material suitability for that use. A material described as “premium” says nothing about its suitability for contact with the contents.

## Canadian labelling: identify the rules for your product

The Competition Bureau's [packaging and labelling guidance](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/labelling/prepackaged-non-food-consumer-products/packaging-and-labelling-requirements) explains the Consumer Packaging and Labelling Act framework for prepackaged non-food consumer products. It identifies product identity, net quantity and dealer identification as key components.

The same guidance lists exempt or differently regulated categories, including food, drugs and medical devices. Do not use a general non-food checklist as a complete label specification for a food or medical product.

For products within the relevant framework, the Bureau's [detailed label guidance](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/labelling/prepackaged-non-food-consumer-products/what-must-be-label) addresses bilingual requirements, placement and text size. Product identity is generally required in English and French; net-quantity information also has bilingual rules, with valid metric symbols treated as bilingual. The guidance describes exemptions and special cases.

The practical design consequence is simple: reserve the space before choosing the front-panel composition. Do not design a beautiful English-only panel and discover at approval that the required French copy has nowhere to go.

Have a qualified reviewer determine which rules apply to the actual product and market. Quebec may have additional language requirements. US distribution needs its own category-specific review. Neither an AI translation nor a printer's approval of file quality establishes legal compliance.

## Do not let environmental styling outrun the evidence

Kraft colour, a leaf icon and the word “green” can create an environmental impression even when the copy is carefully qualified. Review the whole design, not just one sentence.

The Competition Bureau's [environmental-claims guidance, updated in October 2026](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/how-we-foster-competition/education-and-outreach/publications/environmental-claims-and-competition-act-0), says both the literal meaning and general impression matter. It advises clear, specific claims and explains the testing or substantiation requirements that apply to different representations.

Do not describe a package as “eco-friendly” simply because it contains paper. Do not turn a supplier's statement about one component into a claim about the entire product life cycle. If a claim concerns recycled content, identify the component and retain the supporting information. If it concerns an environmental benefit, have the basis and wording reviewed for the actual claim.

Avoid relying on old summaries of the law. The Bureau's current guidance discusses amendments made in March 2026. This guide is not legal advice, and a packaging project needs current review of its own statements.

A useful working document is a claims register: exact wording, what it refers to, supporting evidence, responsible reviewer and approval date. Keep unsupported claims out of the artwork rather than leaving them to be challenged at the final proof.

## Make the barcode a production element

Do not ask a generative image tool to create a barcode that merely looks convincing. Use the correct identifier and standards-based artwork for the relevant retail or logistics application.

[GS1 Canada](https://gs1ca.org/barcodes/) distinguishes barcode formats and explains that sizing, colour, placement and quiet zones affect scanning. It advises against truncating barcodes to make them fit and recommends verification.

Keep the required clear space free of patterns, text and structural interference. A barcode should not be squeezed into the remaining corner after every decorative element has been approved.

Ask the retailer or trading partner which identification and verification requirements apply. Then have the barcode checked in the intended packaging configuration. A successful scan from a screen or an office printout is not the same as verification of the finished pack.

Treat a marketing QR code separately from the product's retail identification. If you include a QR destination, keep it under the brand's control, test it and provide ordinary access to information people need. Do not assume one decorative code replaces the trading partner's required barcode.

## Check the design on a shelf and a phone

Build a comparison using the conditions in which the product will be chosen. For retail, place a physical proof beside representative competing products or a clearly labelled comparison set. For ecommerce, review the actual image crop and small display size.

Ask unfamiliar viewers to identify the product and variant before telling them what you intended. Note confusion between similar colours, abbreviated names or decorative treatments. Revise the hierarchy before adding more explanatory copy.

Check the back and side panels too. The person interested enough to turn the product over needs a readable explanation, not a wall of type shrunk to preserve a decorative pattern.

A fictional three-variant range might use a stable brand position and a large, consistent variant name, with colour as a second cue. That avoids relying entirely on colour differences. The point is to create redundant clarity, not to prescribe a specific palette for every product.

## Proof the material, not just the PDF

Ask the supplier what each proof demonstrates. A digital proof may check wording and placement without guaranteeing the final material or finish. A production sample can answer questions a screen cannot.

Review:

- Spelling, quantities, product names and variant consistency.
- Approved mandatory copy and claims.
- Panel orientation, fold positions and closure areas.
- Barcode quality and placement.
- Colour and finish against the agreed reference.
- Fit with the real contents.
- Assembly, handling, opening and resealing where applicable.
- Shipping or storage performance appropriate to the intended use.

Define suitable tests with the supplier or specialist. Do not invent a drop-test height or a performance standard because it sounds reassuring. A package for a fragile object and one for a soft textile need different validation.

Keep a signed approval record identifying the version and what was checked. Approving “the latest PDF” without a filename or date creates avoidable uncertainty when someone finds another file later.

## Understand the cost of keeping the package in use

Compare quotes at the same quantity, structure, substrate and finishing specification. Ask about setup, tooling, samples, delivery, storage and reorders. A low unit price at an impractical quantity may tie up more cash and space than the business can support.

Consider how the range changes. A large order with seasonal claims or an unconfirmed label statement can become obsolete. Ask whether a stable structure with a replaceable label is appropriate, without assuming that approach works for every product or sales channel.

Keep source files and supplier specifications organised. Record fonts, artwork rights, colour references, dieline versions and the approved copy. Your next print run should not depend on reconstructing the brand from a flattened mockup image.

## A release checklist for the owner

Before authorising production, ask for explicit answers to these questions:

1. Is this the correct structure for the real product and supplier?
2. Has the responsible person approved the applicable label information?
3. Does every claim have an appropriate basis and reviewed wording?
4. Are the identifier, barcode and trading-partner requirements correct?
5. Have the assembled proof and relevant material tests been reviewed?
6. Does the quote match the approved specification and quantity?
7. Is the production version clearly identified and backed up?

These checks are less glamorous than a hero render. They protect the idea you wanted to make in the first place.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can AI design my packaging?

It can help explore artwork and visual directions. The final package still needs correct structure, controlled typography, reviewed label information, production files and appropriate proofs.

### Does every Canadian package need bilingual text?

The answer depends on the product and applicable rules. The Consumer Packaging and Labelling framework generally has bilingual requirements for mandatory information, with exceptions. Food and other regulated categories need their own review.

### Can I call paper packaging eco-friendly?

Do not assume the material name justifies a broad claim. Review the specific statement, the overall impression and the supporting evidence against current Competition Bureau guidance.

### Will a beautiful package sell the product?

Design can help communicate and distinguish it, but it cannot guarantee demand or replace product quality, distribution and a credible offer. Test recognition and understanding rather than inventing a sales-uplift percentage.

## Make the package part of the brand

The strongest packaging is distinctive and easy to use. It carries the identity without losing the product, and it still makes sense after the render becomes a stack of printed boxes.
