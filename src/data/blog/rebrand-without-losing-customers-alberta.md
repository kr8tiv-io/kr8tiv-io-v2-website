# Rebranding without losing customers: a step-by-step for Alberta businesses

Most rebrands do not lose customers because the new logo is ugly. They lose customers because people cannot find the business anymore. The regular who searches the old name gets nothing. The Google profile with years of reviews quietly becomes a closed listing. The old website link in a two-year-old Facebook post leads to an error page. Nobody is angry. They just call someone else.

The fix is mostly order of operations. Check the name before you fall in love with it, make it legal, change the things customers search for in the right sequence, and leave a trail of breadcrumbs from the old name to the new one. This guide walks through each step for an Alberta business, with the actual government rules, Google's actual wording and the fees as they stand today. I checked every source on 24 November 2026, and every one is linked so you can check me back.

It is not legal advice. If a trademark fight is a real possibility, a lawyer or a registered trademark agent is cheaper than a second rebrand.

## First, decide how big this change really is

"Rebrand" covers two very different jobs, and the difference matters more than anything else in this guide.

| | Refresh | Rename |
|---|---|---|
| What changes | Logo, colours, type, photos, voice, website design | The business name itself, usually along with everything in the refresh |
| Government filings | None | Corporate or trade name filings, CRA, business licence |
| Google Business Profile | Same profile, new photos and logo | Depends on Google's rebranding test (step 5) |
| Website address | Usually stays the same | Often moves to a new domain, which means redirects |
| Risk of losing customers | Low, if the old look is retired carefully | Real, unless you plan the trail from old name to new |

A refresh is a new outfit. A rename is a new passport. Plenty of businesses that think they need a new name actually need a better identity on the name they already have, and that is cheaper and keeps every bit of recognition you have earned. If you are still deciding, [logo or brand](/blog/logo-vs-brand-small-business/) is a good place to sort out which problem you have.

Big brands get this wrong too, which is comforting. In October 2010, Gap swapped its blue box logo for a new one and dropped it within about a week, after what the company called ["an outpouring of comments from customers"](https://www.gapinc.com/en-us/articles/2010/10/gap-listens-to-customers-and-will-keep-classic-blu) in favour of the old one. In 2009, PepsiCo [brought back the old Tropicana carton](https://www.npr.org/2009/02/23/101024317/consumers-reject-new-tropicana-carton) after shoppers complained that the redesign did not look like the juice they bought. Neither change touched the name. People had simply learned to find those brands by a look, and the look moved.

Your customers have learned to find you by something too. Step one is working out what.

## Step 1: List everything customers already know you by

Before anyone opens a design file, write down every place your current name, logo, phone number or web address appears. This list becomes your rollout plan, your budget and your to-do list, so be thorough and a little paranoid.

* **Search and maps.** Google Business Profile, Bing Places, Apple Maps, and any directory that lists you.
* **Your website.** The domain, page titles, the footer, contact pages, the favicon, any business details in the code, and the email addresses that run on that domain.
* **Social profiles.** Facebook, LinkedIn, Instagram and anywhere else with your name, handle and profile image.
* **Paperwork.** Invoices, quotes, contracts, receipts, letterhead, email signatures, business cards and warranty cards.
* **Physical things.** Storefront signs, window vinyl, vehicles, uniforms, packaging, trade show gear and the sticker on the back of the equipment you install.
* **Accounts and registrations.** Corporate registry, CRA, your municipal business licence, the bank, insurance, suppliers, and industry associations or the chamber.
* **Things you forgot.** The voicemail greeting. The QR code on the menu. The listing on the supplier's dealer locator. The sponsor banner at the rink.

Then mark which items customers use to *find* you and which they only see *after* they found you. The first group is where customers get lost, so it gets changed first and most carefully. The second group can roll out on a schedule.

## Step 2: Check the new name before anyone gets attached to it

The most expensive rebrand is the one you have to do twice. Before a designer touches the new name, run four checks.

**Alberta business names.** The Province is blunt about this. Alberta's [business name page](https://www.alberta.ca/register-business-name) says names "don't have to be unique," but if yours is the same as or similar to an existing business name, corporation name or trademark, "the owners could take your business to court; you may have to change your name or pay damages." It also says registering a business name "does not grant any right of ownership of the name." A trade name also cannot end in "Limited," "Incorporated" or "Corporation," or Ltd., Inc. or Corp., because that would imply a corporation.

**A NUANS report.** NUANS is the name search that lists similar registered business, corporation and trademark names. Alberta recommends one for a trade name and requires an Alberta NUANS report for most corporate name changes. More on that in step 3.

**Trademarks.** Search the [Canadian Trademarks Database](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/cipo/trademark-search/srch) for your new name and close variations, especially in your own industry. The Canadian Intellectual Property Office puts one point very plainly: "Registering your domain name or incorporating your business does not give you any trademark rights" ([CIPO, Learn Trademarks](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/learn-trademarks-canadian-intellectual-property-office)). A registered trademark gives you the exclusive right to use the mark across Canada for 10 years, renewable. Filing online costs [$491.06 for the first class of goods or services and $149.04 for each additional class](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canadian-intellectual-property-office/en/trademarks/fees-trademarks) in 2026, rising to $499.41 and $151.57 on 1 January 2027.

**The internet.** Check that you can get a sensible domain, ideally a .ca or .com without hyphens, and the same handle on the social networks you actually use. Then say the name out loud on the phone to someone who has never heard it. If they cannot spell it after hearing it once, neither can the customer typing it into Google.

A name that passes all four checks is not guaranteed safe, but a name that fails any one of them is a problem you found for free.

## Step 3: Make it legal in Alberta, then tell the government twice more

The filing depends on how your business is set up.

**If you are a corporation.** Alberta handles a name change as an amendment to your articles. The Province's [amendment page](https://www.alberta.ca/amend-notices-for-a-corporation-cooperative-or-non-profit) lays out the steps: get an Alberta NUANS report for the new name, fill out the Articles of Amendment, then take the articles, the NUANS report, valid ID and payment to an authorized service provider, which in practice means a registry agent. The NUANS report reserves the name for 90 days, and the original report must be less than 91 days old when you file. Changing to a number name, such as 1234567 Alberta Ltd., needs no NUANS report. The government fee to change the name of an Alberta corporation is $53.05, according to the [Registry Agent Product Catalogue](https://open.alberta.ca/publications/6041328) effective 1 October 2026. The agent adds its own service charge, which varies, so it is worth calling two or three.

**If you are a sole proprietor, a partnership, or a corporation using a trade name.** A trade name is the name you use when you do business under something other than your own legal name or your corporation's legal name. The same catalogue lists registering a trade name at a $10.61 government fee, plus the agent's service charge, and dissolving a trade name or partnership at no government fee. There is no separate "rename" line for a trade name, so plan on registering the new one and dissolving the old one once you are done with it.

**If you do business in British Columbia, Saskatchewan or Manitoba.** Alberta's page notes that an Alberta corporation's name change also has to be registered in those provinces if you operate there.

Then tell the government twice more:

* **The CRA.** Your operating name is the name you advertise under. The CRA says that if it changes, ["you must advise the Canada Revenue Agency of the change"](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/business-registration/maintain-business/change-operating-name.html), which you can do online in My Business Account, by phone or by letter. For a corporation's legal name, the CRA says changes must be updated with the incorporating authority first, and then with the CRA.
* **Your municipal business licence.** In Edmonton, a change of business name is on the City's list of [changes you must report](https://www.edmonton.ca/business_economy/licences_permits/update-your-business-licence), using the Business Licence Account Update Request. If the City cannot update the licence, staff will tell you to apply for a new one. A change of legal entity or owner's name needs a new licence outright. Other Alberta municipalities have their own rules, so check yours.

After that: the bank, your insurer, suppliers, payment processors, any professional regulator and the WCB account. None of them will find out on their own, and the bank in particular gets twitchy about cheques made out to a name it has never heard of.

## Step 4: Change the real world before you change Google

This sounds backwards, but it is how Google's rules work. Google's [guidelines for representing your business](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) say your name "should reflect your business's real-world name, as used consistently on your storefront, website, stationery, and as known to customers." Google wants the Business Profile to match the world, not lead it.

So the things a person or a Google reviewer could check should change first, or at least on the same day: the website, the sign or window lettering, and the documents a customer might see. If Google asks for proof, you want the new name already living on things with your address on them.

The same guidelines say to keep extra words out of the name field. Marketing taglines, keywords and service lists do not belong there. "Northline Plumbing," not "Northline Plumbing, Best Furnace Repair Edmonton." That rule also kills the obvious trick of writing "Northline Plumbing (formerly Smith Plumbing)" in the name. Put the "formerly" line in your description and posts instead.

## Step 5: The Google Business Profile step that decides whether you keep your reviews

This is the step most rebrand guides skip, and it is the one that costs the most.

Google has a specific rule for name changes. In the same [guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en), under "Rebranding," Google says a profile "may be eligible for rebranding (defined as an eligible name change without creating a new Business Profile) if you make a minor name change, in which the proper nouns and services described in the business name remain unchanged and the business category remains unchanged." Businesses with multiple locations may also be eligible when the name changes.

If you pass, you edit the name on your existing profile and the profile, with its history, carries on. Google reviews profile edits before they go live, according to its [edit your Business Profile](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617?hl=en) help page.

If you do not pass, Google is just as clear: the business "is considered a new business. You must mark the existing Business Profile as closed and then create a new Business Profile with your new business name." Google's guidelines do not say your reviews follow you to the new profile. Plan as if they do not.

Here is what that looks like with made-up names:

* **Smith Plumbing Ltd. to Smith Plumbing.** Same proper noun, same service, same category. Very likely an eligible rebrand.
* **Smith Plumbing to Northline Plumbing.** The proper noun changed. By Google's definition, that is a new business.
* **Smith Plumbing to Smith Home Services, now selling roofing too.** The services in the name changed and the category probably will. Also a new business.

That gives you a real decision to make *before* you pick a name. If your Google reviews are a big part of how customers choose you, and for most Alberta trades and local shops they are, a name that keeps its proper noun may be worth more than a cleverer one that does not. If the new name is worth starting over for, go in with a plan: tell your best customers early, ask for reviews the honest way once the new profile is live, and read [the rules for Google reviews in Canada](/blog/google-reviews-canada-rules/) before anyone offers a discount in exchange for stars.

Two more Google rules to keep in mind. Google says there "should only be one profile per business," so do not open a second profile for the new name while the old one is still active. And if anything goes sideways, Google's guidelines point you to its support team and the Business Profile Community. If the profile disappears altogether, [why a Business Profile does not show](/blog/google-business-profile-not-showing/) covers the usual causes.

## Step 6: Move the website without losing your rankings

If you keep your domain and only change the design, the name and the content, this is the easy version. Update page titles, the footer, the contact page, the favicon, your structured business details and your email signatures, and resubmit your sitemap in Search Console.

If the domain changes too, this is a site move, and Google's [guide to moving a site with URL changes](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) is the manual. The short version:

1. **Map every old URL to its new home.** Every page, every image people link to, every PDF.
2. **Use permanent server-side redirects,** such as 301s, from each old URL to the matching new one. Google says 301 and other permanent redirects "don't cause a loss in PageRank." A redirect that dumps everything on the new homepage is the lazy version, and it wastes the work you did on individual pages.
3. **Update your own links and tags.** Internal links, canonical tags and sitemaps should all point at the new URLs.
4. **Tell Google about the move.** Verify both old and new sites in Search Console, then use the [Change of Address tool](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9370220?hl=en) for a domain change.
5. **Keep the redirects for a long time.** The site move guide says to keep them "for as long as possible, generally at least 1 year." The Change of Address page says to maintain them for at least 180 days and to keep paying for the old domain "for at least a year to prevent others from buying and using your abandoned domain for malicious purposes."

Google also advises changing "only one thing at a time." If you can, move the domain first and redesign later, or redesign first on the old domain and move after. And it warns to "expect temporary fluctuation in site ranking," with medium-sized sites taking "a few weeks or more" before Google mostly shows the new URLs. Plan the launch for your quiet season, not the week before your busiest one.

Two small things that save real customers: keep the old email addresses forwarding to the new ones for at least as long as the redirects, and keep the old phone number. A phone number is the one piece of your old brand that nobody has to relearn.

## Step 7: Update the listings and the socials

Once Google is sorted, work through the rest of the list from step 1, starting with the places that send you the most customers.

* **Bing Places and Apple Maps.** Update your listing in [Bing Places](https://www.bingplaces.com/) and claim or update your place in [Apple Business Connect](https://businessconnect.apple.com/). Apple Maps is the map app that comes on every iPhone, so its listing matters even if you never open it yourself.
* **Facebook.** A Page name change is a request that Meta reviews before it applies, and Meta has [Page name guidelines](https://www.facebook.com/help/519912414718764) that rule out things like slogans and the word "official." Start it early. The steps are on Meta's [change your Page name](https://www.facebook.com/help/271607792873806) help page.
* **LinkedIn.** LinkedIn sorts name changes into [minimal and significant changes](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a566295). A Page with a small number of associated employees can usually edit the name itself, while bigger or verified Pages have to contact LinkedIn. Two catches from LinkedIn's own page: the Page URL "isn't automatically changed when you change your page name," and changing the Page name does not change the job titles on your staff's profiles, so ask them to update.
* **Directories and memberships.** The chamber, trade associations, supplier dealer locators, Yelp and industry directories. Use the exact same name, address and phone everywhere. Consistent details across the web make you easier to trust and easier to find.

Keep a simple spreadsheet: where, login, date changed, date confirmed. You will need it again when a customer says "I saw your old name on..." and you get to fix one more.

## Step 8: Roll out the physical brand in a sensible order

Signs, vehicles and uniforms are where a rebrand gets expensive, so stage them.

**Storefront signs.** In Edmonton, Zoning Bylaw section 7.120, subsection 8.15, says that [changing the copy of an existing sign](https://zoningbylaw.edmonton.ca/part-7-administrative-and-interpretive-clauses/development-permits/7120-no-development-permit-required) with a valid development permit does not need a development permit, as long as it does not change the use, location, size, height, general design of the overall sign, or method of illumination. In plain terms, a new face in the same lit cabinet may be simple. A bigger sign, a new spot or new lighting puts you back in the permit queue. [Edmonton's sign permit guide](/blog/edmonton-sign-permits/) has the fees and timelines. Other municipalities have their own bylaws.

**Vehicles.** A wrapped truck is a moving sign, and it is one of the places people see your name most often. Schedule wraps around your slow weeks. If you are planning a full wrap, have the design done for the vehicle, not stretched onto it. [How vehicle wrap design works](/blog/ai-vehicle-wrap-design-edmonton/) goes through it.

**Uniforms, stationery and packaging.** Use up the old stock where nobody new sees it, and order the new stock in sensible quantities. Nobody needs 5,000 business cards in a name that is three weeks old.

A practical rule: change the things that help new customers find you first, the things returning customers see second, and the things only you see last.

## Step 9: Tell customers before, during and after

Rebrands that keep customers are boringly well announced. Tell people more than feels necessary.

* **Before launch.** Email your customer list, tell your regulars in person, and brief your staff so they can answer "did you get bought out?" with a straight face.
* **On launch day.** Post on your Google profile, Facebook and LinkedIn, put a short note on the website, and change the phone greeting. Something like "Smith Plumbing is now Northline Plumbing. Same crew, same number" does the job.
* **For a few months after.** Keep a "formerly Smith Plumbing" line in your website footer, your Google description, your email signature and on invoices. This is a judgment call rather than a rule, but six to twelve months is a reasonable bridge for most local businesses. Long enough for the people who only call once a year to see it.

Explain *why* if the reason is good. A new partner, a bigger service area, a name that was always getting confused with someone else. People are generous when they understand the reason. They are suspicious when a familiar business suddenly looks like a stranger.

## The ways rebrands quietly lose customers

Most of these are avoidable with a checklist and a calendar.

* **A new name that fails Google's rebrand test,** picked without anyone realizing it would mean starting a new profile from zero reviews.
* **A second Google profile** opened for the new name while the old one is still live.
* **A new domain with no redirects,** or redirects that all point at the homepage.
* **Letting the old domain expire** a few months after launch.
* **A new sign that went up before the website changed,** or the other way around, so nothing matches for weeks.
* **A logo that only works big.** If it falls apart on a phone screen, an embroidered chest patch or a profile picture, it is not finished.
* **Forgetting the boring places,** such as invoices, the voicemail and the supplier's dealer list, which are often exactly where loyal customers look.

## A simple rebrand timeline

Every business is different, but this is a realistic shape for a small Alberta company with one location.

| When | What happens |
|---|---|
| Weeks 1 to 2 | Touchpoint list, name checks (Alberta, NUANS, CIPO, domain, handles), decide refresh or rename |
| Weeks 2 to 6 | Identity design: logo suite, colours, type, guidelines and the key applications |
| Weeks 5 to 7 | Registry filing, CRA and business licence updates, bank and insurance; website built on the new name |
| Launch week | Website live with redirects, Google profile updated or replaced, signs or window vinyl, announcements |
| Weeks 8 to 16 | Listings, vehicles, uniforms and stock roll over; watch Search Console and the phone |
| Months 3 to 12 | Keep redirects, forwarding and "formerly" lines in place; then retire the old trade name |

If you only remember one row, remember the last one. The rebrand is finished when the old name stops getting searched, not when the new sign goes up.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will I lose my Google reviews if I change my business name?

Not if Google treats the change as a rebrand. Google lets you edit the name on your existing Business Profile when the change is minor, meaning the proper nouns and the services in the name stay the same and the business category does not change. If the new name does not meet that test, Google considers it a new business and asks you to mark the old profile closed and create a new one. Google's guidelines do not say reviews move to the new profile, so plan as if they do not.

### How do I change my business name in Alberta?

A corporation gets an Alberta NUANS report for the new name, then files Articles of Amendment through a registry agent. The government fee is $53.05 plus the agent's service charge, and the NUANS report must be less than 91 days old. A sole proprietor or partnership registers the new trade name through a registry agent for a $10.61 government fee plus the service charge. Then update the CRA and your municipal business licence.

### Do I need a new sign permit in Edmonton if I rebrand?

Not always. Edmonton's Zoning Bylaw says changing the copy on an existing sign that has a valid development permit does not need a new development permit, as long as the use, location, size, height, overall design and method of illumination stay the same. Change any of those, such as a bigger cabinet or new lighting, and you need a permit.

### How long should I keep redirects from my old website?

Google recommends keeping redirects from old URLs to new ones for as long as possible, and generally at least one year. If you use the Change of Address tool in Search Console, Google says to keep the redirects for at least 180 days and to keep paying for the old domain for at least a year so nobody else can buy it.
