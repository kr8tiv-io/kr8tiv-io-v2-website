# How AI can make your business more creative, without making you look like everyone else

Ask a model for a logo for an Edmonton contractor and tell it to make the thing pop. You will get a mountain, a hard hat, and a maple leaf shaking hands with a wrench. Your competitor asked yesterday. They got the same handshake, in a slightly different blue.

That is the whole trick, and the whole trap. AI has swallowed the public internet, and its favourite flavour is average. Average is fast. Average is also how a good company starts to look like a template with a phone number taped on.

Used with a short brief and a strict editor, the same tool is the best junior you will ever have. It does not get tired, it does not get precious, and it will give you twelve options before your coffee cools. It will not know which one is yours. That part is still a person. In this studio, that person is Matt.

This is for owners in Edmonton, Sherwood Park, St. Albert, Spruce Grove, and the rest of Alberta who are typing some version of "how can AI help my business" into a search box at 10pm. The same method works anywhere in Canada. The local part is the photo of your actual door, and the fact that a lot of your customers will see the work from a truck, in the dark, in February.

## Can AI design my logo?

It can sketch directions. It cannot hand you a finished logo.

A logo is a name, set in letters someone drew on purpose, that still works on a truck door, a website tab, an invoice, and a hat. A generated picture of a logo is a picture. It usually misspells the name, invents a symbol you do not own, and falls apart the moment a printer asks for a vector file.

Adobe's [Text to Vector Graphic in Illustrator](https://www.adobe.com/products/illustrator/text-to-vector-graphic.html) is one of the few generators that gives you editable paths, which makes it useful for exploring a mark. Editable is not the same as finished. The wordmark still has to be drawn, spaced, and checked by a person who can spell your name.

If you want the longer version of what a logo is and is not, read [logo or brand](/blog/logo-vs-brand-small-business/). Short version: a logo is one file. A brand is what people recognise when the logo is covered. AI is decent at the file-shaped part of the exploration. It is hopeless at the recognition part, because recognition is built from your real customers, not from the average of every logo on the internet.

## Can AI write my website?

It can write a first draft that sounds like a website. That is a lower bar than it looks.

The draft will call you trusted, committed, and a leader in your space. So will the draft it writes for the shop across the road. Google's guidance on [generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) is blunt about why. These models do not look your business up. They predict a likely next sentence. Likely, for a local company, is a pile of sentences that could belong to anyone.

Give it the facts it cannot know, or do not bother:

- What you actually sell, in the words a customer uses on the phone.
- Who it is for, and who it is not for.
- The neighbourhoods you will drive to, and the ones you will not.
- One proof you can stand behind this month. A photo, a method, a number you measured. Not "the best in Edmonton."
- The words you refuse. If you would not say it at the counter, it does not go on the page.

Then delete half. The page that gets the call is usually the one that answers a specific fear. For a lot of Alberta businesses that fear is "will this look cheap, take six months, or lock me into a template I cannot change." That question has its own note: [when a custom website pays off](/blog/website-that-earns-its-keep-alberta/).

## Will AI replace a designer or a marketing agency?

It replaces the blank page. It does not replace the decision.

The decision is small and annoying, which is why people try to hand it to a chatbot. What should a stranger believe in the first three seconds? Which claim is true? Which picture is your shop, and which picture is a hotel in another country with your name pasted on? A model will answer all three with total confidence. Confidence is not a source.

I use models in the studio for the pile: name directions, headline options, rough layout moods, the ugly first pass of a paragraph. Then I throw most of it away. The owners who get a good result are the ones who can look at option four and say "that one sounds like us, and the other eleven sound like a brochure." If nobody in the company can say that, a model will not create the taste. It will multiply the confusion at a higher speed.

## How do I use AI for ads without sounding like a robot?

Ask for lines a stranger can believe one at a time. Google will split them up whether you like it or not.

A responsive search ad takes up to 15 headlines and 4 descriptions. Headlines support up to 30 characters. Descriptions support up to 90. You need at least 3 headlines and 2 descriptions before the ad can run. Google's help page on [responsive search ads](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7684791) is the source, and it is worth reading because the mixer is the whole point. The headlines get combined. A headline that only makes sense after the one before it will eventually run alone, and it will sound unhinged.

That constraint is a gift. "Edmonton roofs, booked this month" can stand alone. "And also we care" cannot. Ask the model for 15 headlines under 30 characters, using only the facts you typed above the prompt. Then delete any line you would not say to a person who is about to pay you. You will usually keep five. Five true lines beat fifteen decorative ones.

Two local filters, before you spend a dollar:

- Speed. On Whyte Avenue or 124 Street, someone can stop. On the Whitemud or the Henday, they will not. If the idea needs a paragraph, it is a page, not an ad, and not a fascia.
- Dark. After about 4:30 in November, a lot of Edmonton sees your work as a small bright rectangle on a phone, or a sign in headlights. If the clever version dies in that light, ship the plain one.

The longer version of spending the money is [local advertising in Edmonton](/blog/local-advertising-edmonton-alberta/) and [how a campaign is built](/blog/marketing-campaign-edmonton/). The creative still has to be true, or the spend is a very efficient way to be ignored.

## Will Google penalise AI writing?

Google does not ban the tool. It bans pages that waste a person's time.

The same document says using generative tools to spin up many pages without adding value can count as scaled content abuse. One useful page that a person checked is not that. Fifty pages that swap "Edmonton" for "Sherwood Park" and change nothing else are the thing they are describing. Do not publish the fifty. A page earns the enquiry when it has a job of its own, which is the whole point of [the blogging note](/blog/blog-that-ranks-and-brings-leads/).

Two more rules from Google, in plain language:

- Fact-check it. The model will invent a price, a neighbourhood, a review, and a founding year with a straight face. If you did not measure it, it does not go on the site. This applies to the title, the meta description, and the image alt text too, because those show up in search.
- Be worth citing. Their guide to [AI features in Search](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) says there is no special trick, and no special schema, required to be eligible for AI Overviews. The page has to be indexed, and eligible to show a snippet. Original judgement is the part a search system cannot get from a thousand copies of the same article. [Helpful content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) is the standard they publish. A first-hand decision from work you actually do beats a list of tips anyone could have generated.

## What should I try this week in Edmonton?

Forty-five minutes. One offer. A photo of the real place. Here is the drill. It is a drill, not a client. If you run a cabinet shop in Sherwood Park, use your own name and your own door. Do not borrow a neighbour's.

**Minutes 0 to 5. One sentence.**
Write what you sell, who it is for, and the next step. "We build painted shaker kitchens for houses in Sherwood Park, and you can book a measure." If you cannot write that sentence, stop. A model will invent a personality for you, and you will spend the month sounding like a catalogue.

**Minutes 5 to 15. Five inputs, on paper.**
Three phrases customers actually say. Not the phrases you wish they said. One photo of the door, the truck, or the product, taken in the light you really have. The words you refuse. Do not paste a customer's address, a private quote, or an unreleased drawing into a public model. That is not research. That is a leak.

**Minutes 15 to 25. Twelve directions, not one.**
Use the prompt in the next section. You are buying a pile, not a verdict. If all twelve sound the same, your inputs were too thin. Add a fact and run it again.

**Minutes 25 to 35. Score them like a cynic.**

| Test | It passes when | It fails when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stranger | Someone who has never met you can say what you sell | It could be any shop on the same street |
| Out loud | You would say it at the counter | You would only say it in a caption |
| Photo | It still looks right on a picture of your real door, truck, or product | It only looks right inside the generated scene |
| Truth | Every claim is something you can show this month | It borrowed a result, a city, or a superlative |
| Winter | It still reads on a phone, in a mitt, after dark | It needs a lecture and a sunny afternoon |

Kill at least nine. If you cannot kill nine, the prompt was too polite.

**Minutes 35 to 45. Put the survivor in four places.**
The first line of the homepage. The description on your Google Business Profile. Three ad headlines under 30 characters, each true on its own. One line for the truck or the sign, checked against the real vehicle or the real building. A generated storefront that moves your door is not a design. The [signage guide](/blog/storefront-sign-design-edmonton/) covers the part where a picture has to survive a wall.

## A prompt you can paste tonight

Replace the brackets. Delete any line the model adds that you did not supply. If it flatters you, that line is lying.

> You are helping a small business in [neighbourhood, Edmonton or another Alberta city] think of creative directions. The business sells [offer in one sentence]. Customers say things like [three real phrases]. We refuse these words: [list]. Here is what is true this month: [one to three facts]. Using only those facts, give me 12 directions. Four names or taglines, four homepage opening lines, and four ad headlines under 30 characters. Make the 12 clearly different from each other. Do not invent reviews, awards, years in business, prices, or guarantees. Mark any line that only works if a fact is missing. Then stop.

Read the output out loud. The line that makes you wince, and then laugh, is often the honest one. The line that sounds expensive and smooth is usually the average of everyone else. Keep the wince.

For pictures, add one more constraint: "Use the attached photo of our real location. Do not move the door, do not add a second storey, and do not change the street." If the model renovates your building for you, keep the colour idea and throw the architecture away. We wrote the production version of this for [signs](/blog/ai-signage-design-edmonton/), [storefront layouts](/blog/ai-storefront-layout-design-edmonton/), and [vehicle wraps](/blog/ai-vehicle-wrap-design-edmonton/). The rule is the same on a website hero. A pretty lie about your building is still a lie.

## Where a person still has to show up

AI is a multiplier. It multiplies whatever you put in. Put in a vague mood and you get a vague mood, twelve times, with better lighting. Put in a true offer and a real photo and you get something you can edit.

A person still has to:

- Spell the name.
- Pick the line a customer can repeat.
- Check the file a printer, a fabricator, or a website can actually use.
- Refuse the superlative.
- Own the result when someone calls.

That is the work. The model is the pile of paper on the desk before the work starts.
