# How to attract customers with signage they can actually use

Good signage helps a suitable customer recognise the business, understand the offer and find the entrance. Make those decisions easy before adding a slogan, a lighting effect or a screen. Then test the design in the real viewing conditions and measure useful enquiries rather than promising an automatic sales lift.

A sign does not need to explain your entire business. It needs to do the part of the selling job that belongs at that location. The name above a shop, the information on its door and the message beside a product can work together without repeating the same crowded layout.

For an Edmonton storefront, start outside. The best place to diagnose a weak sign is often the route a first-time visitor takes from the street or parking area—not the designer's monitor.

## Diagnose the problem before replacing the sign

Walk the customer journey with someone who has never visited. Give them the business name and address, but do not tell them which door to use. Ask them to describe where they become uncertain.

Look for different problems:

- **Recognition:** the sign is difficult to identify among neighbouring units.
- **Understanding:** the name is readable, but the visitor cannot tell what the business offers.
- **Arrival:** the business is obvious, but the entrance or unit is not.
- **Reassurance:** visitors cannot see whether the business is open or suitable for their needs.
- **Action:** people want to enquire, but the contact route is missing or unclear.

Those problems need different responses. A new illuminated logo will not necessarily fix an entrance hidden around the side of the building. A long service list will not help if the name is too small to read.

Photograph the confusing moments safely from pedestrian positions. Record what was visible, what the visitor expected and what information was missing. This turns “the sign does not work” into a specific design brief.

## Give each surface one main job

Treat the storefront as a system rather than one large advertisement. Decide where a person will be looking when each question arises.

| Surface | Main question to answer | Information that often belongs elsewhere |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary identity sign | Is this the business I am looking for? | Full service menus and lengthy promotional copy |
| Entrance or door information | Where do I go, and can I enter now? | A second version of the entire brand story |
| Window or pedestrian message | Is this relevant to me? | Detail that requires a long explanation while blocking the view |
| Interior directional sign | Where is the next destination? | Decorative messaging that competes with directions |
| Product display | What is this, and what should I know before buying? | Unrelated brand claims |

This is a planning framework, not a formula for every premises. A clinic, restaurant and trade showroom have different visitor needs. Start with the decisions, then choose the surfaces.

Keep temporary promotions separate from permanent identification. It should be possible to change an offer without redesigning the main sign or leaving an expired poster taped over it.

## Choose the words before choosing the effects

Use the business name people actually know. Add a short service descriptor when the name alone leaves a reasonable visitor guessing. Avoid using a tagline as the only explanation of what is sold.

For a fictional business called Northline, “Northline” could mean almost anything. “Northline — Commercial Electrical” gives a suitable buyer more context. This is an illustrative name, not a client or a recommendation to use that identity.

Prioritise the information in order:

1. The name that needs to be recognised.
2. The service or category if it is not already clear.
3. The arrival cue or contact action needed at that viewing position.

If the proposal contains six equally prominent messages, ask which one the viewer should remember. Do not solve the argument by shrinking all six until they fit.

Check every claim. “Open late,” “same-day” and “all makes” describe operating commitments. Someone needs to keep them accurate. A permanent sign is a poor home for a promise the business cannot reliably maintain.

## Test readability without pretending there is one magic number

A useful test uses a scaled proposal in a photograph of the actual location. It should include the neighbouring visual context, not just a clean logo on an empty wall.

Ask the designer to show the main approach views. Check whether the typeface, spacing, contrast and hierarchy survive at the apparent size customers will see. A beautiful hairline stroke in a close-up may disappear when the whole shopfront is shown.

Avoid treating a generic letter-height calculator as the complete answer. Viewing distance is important, but so are the letterforms, lighting, angle, background and time available to read. Your project needs a site-specific judgement.

Run a simple observation exercise:

- Show the mockup without naming the intended message.
- Ask the viewer to identify the name and service.
- Ask where they would enter.
- Record their words before explaining the design.
- Change the unclear part and repeat the exercise with another person.

This is a design check, not a statistically representative study. It is still more useful than a room of people saying they like the colour.

## Contrast is a relationship, not a fashionable colour choice

Review the mark against the actual surface and surrounding signs. A bright accent on a dark wall can work differently from the same accent on glass, brushed metal or a lit acrylic face.

Keep the most important lettering separate from busy imagery. If the artwork needs a detailed background, put the name in a calmer area rather than making every element fight for attention.

Review both the day appearance and the intended after-dark appearance. Ask how illumination affects the edges of letters and whether reflections or glare obscure useful detail. A night render with the rest of the street artificially darkened can exaggerate the result.

Where colour is important to the identity, approve an appropriate physical sample. Use that approved reference in the production record. Do not expect a phone display to settle a material or colour disagreement between suppliers.

## Design the entrance as carefully as the name

A visitor who recognises the business can still arrive at the wrong door. Look at unit numbers, accessible routes, shared entrances, pickup locations and the path from parking or transit.

Do not hide essential information in a graphic treatment. Opening hours should be readable where a visitor can safely stop. If appointments are required, say so accurately. If the entrance is around the corner, place the cue before the point where people take the wrong route.

Review door graphics with the actual door in use. Handles, push plates, glazing bars and open-door positions can interrupt wording. Make sure a promotional message does not obscure information people need for arrival and safety.

Required building and accessibility signage is a separate responsibility from decorative branding. Have the appropriate professional confirm what is required rather than assuming a custom graphic can replace a regulated sign.

## Keep the sign connected to the business people find online

Use a consistent real-world identity across the storefront, website and eligible business listings. Google's [Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177) say the name should reflect the real-world business name used consistently on signage, the website and stationery. They also require accurate address or service-area information.

That does not mean adding a list of Edmonton keywords to the business name. Google's guidance explicitly restricts unnecessary information in the name field. Keep services and location information in the appropriate places rather than inventing a longer name for search.

A useful website supports the sign by answering the questions the sign cannot. Give visitors a relevant [service page](https://kr8tiv.io/services/web-design-edmonton/), clear contact information and an accurate explanation of the next step. Show current exterior or entrance photographs where they genuinely help customers recognise the premises.

A short readable domain can support recall. A QR code can be useful on a surface where people can safely stand and scan. Neither should be the only route to essential information. Do not design a roadside sign around asking drivers to use a phone.

## Match the material to the communication job

Compare materials and construction after the message and placement are clear. Ask the fabricator to specify the actual product, finish, mounting and exposure suitability rather than quoting an unexplained “premium” option.

A flat panel, dimensional letters and illuminated channel letters are not merely price levels. They change how the identity occupies the building and appears after dark. A window graphic and a projecting sign also serve different viewing directions.

Request maintenance information with the quote. Ask how damaged components are replaced, how the sign is cleaned and what access equipment would be needed for repair. A difficult service location can affect the real cost of keeping the sign presentable.

For a changing display, agree on who updates it. A screen showing an old promotion is not inherently better than a printed panel with current information.

## Include Edmonton permits in the schedule

The City's [permanent-sign page](https://www.edmonton.ca/city_government/bylaws/permanent-signs) says signs on private property require a sign permit except for the exemptions listed in Section 7.120 of the Zoning Bylaw. An electrical connection also requires an electrical permit. Confirm the requirements for your location and sign type before installation.

The City asks for project information such as dimensions, elevations, materials, illumination and support details, with requirements varying by sign type. Some proposals require engineering information. Do not assume a small sign, a window treatment or a landlord approval automatically settles every requirement.

Build the opening schedule around the actual approval and production sequence. Identify who owns the site measurements, landlord review, application, specialist design, production proof and installation arrangements. Confirm dependencies before promising an installation date.

Businesses elsewhere in Alberta need their own municipality's guidance. A useful local article should explain when location changes the advice—not replace one city name with another and imply the rules are identical.

## Measure the right outcome

A new sign can support recognition and enquiries, but a before-and-after sales change does not prove the sign caused it. Opening hours, season, promotions, staffing and other marketing may also change.

Choose a few practical observations before installation:

- How often do first-time visitors report difficulty finding the entrance?
- How many relevant enquiries mention seeing the business while passing?
- Are people arriving at the wrong unit or contacting the wrong business?
- Does the website receive useful visits from a clearly identified sign link?
- Do staff spend less time giving corrective arrival directions?

Ask “How did you hear about us?” without forcing every answer into a single channel. A customer may see the sign, receive a referral and later search the name. Keep those details rather than claiming perfect attribution.

If you use a distinct URL or campaign link, test that it lands on the correct page and remains under your control. Do not build the measurement system around a third-party short link that nobody can maintain.

## A copyable sign brief

Use this before asking for a quote. Replace the prompts with facts from your own business; they are planning fields, not ready-to-publish claims.

**Business and location:** real name, address or site, existing sign restrictions and landlord contact.

**Primary job:** the one decision the sign should make easier.

**Audience and approach:** who is looking, where they approach from and where they can stop.

**Message hierarchy:** name, descriptor and any arrival or contact cue.

**Assets:** approved logo files, typefaces, colour references and usage permissions.

**Physical context:** photographs, measurements, obstructions, surface condition and intended illumination.

**Responsibilities:** design, measurements, approvals, engineering, fabrication, electrical work, installation and maintenance.

**Review:** how the design will be checked at realistic scale and who approves the final proof.

**Measurement:** the customer confusion or enquiry outcome you will observe after installation.

The brief does not need to be elaborate. It needs to prevent expensive assumptions from hiding inside a good-looking mockup.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a small-business sign say?

Lead with the recognisable business name and add a short service descriptor when needed. Put detailed offers, hours and contact information on surfaces where people have time to read them.

### Will a bigger sign bring more customers?

Not automatically. First check the message, contrast, viewing direction, entrance cues and permitted size. A larger version of a confusing layout may remain confusing.

### Should I put a QR code on my storefront sign?

Use it only where people can safely stop, read and scan. Keep an ordinary contact route available. A QR code should not be the main action for someone driving past.

### Can signage improve my Google ranking?

A real-world identity can support consistent business information and customer recognition. A sign does not, by itself, guarantee a ranking increase or an AI-search citation.
