# When an Alberta trades business needs a rebrand, and when a logo tweak is enough

You need a rebrand when the current identity is making it harder to get hired. You need a logo tweak when the name still works, people already recognise you, and the files are what is letting you down. Most Edmonton and Alberta trades are closer to the second problem than they think, and a few are long past it.

“Branding” gets sold as a personality workshop. For a crew that lives on job sites, it is more practical than that. It is the name, the mark, the colours and the words that have to survive a truck door, a hi-vis chest, a muddy invoice and a phone-sized website.

KR8TIV’s [branding work](/services/branding/) starts with what you sell and who should hire you. This guide is here so you can decide the scale of the job before anyone redraws a mascot you did not need.

## How can you tell the identity is costing you work?

Watch for confusion, not for boredom with your colours.

Typical signs:

* Customers call you by a competitor’s name, or by a nickname you never chose.
* The logo looks acceptable on a screen and falls apart on a card, a stencil or a vehicle.
* Every supplier builds a slightly different version because the only file you have is a faded PNG.
* The website, the truck and the invoices look like three different companies.
* You have changed what you sell, and the old name or language no longer tells the truth.

A fictional Spruce Grove landscaper who started with mowing and now does full hardscaping may still be introducing themselves as “the lawn guy.” That is not a typography problem. That is an identity that lagged behind the offer.

The opposite case is common too. A solid Edmonton electrical company has a recognisable wordmark, but the only usable file is a photo of the van. They do not need a new personality. They need a clean vector, a simple colour rule, and a typeface that still reads at a distance.

If you are unsure, ask two people who do not work for you to describe the business from the truck and the website alone. If they cannot, write down where they hesitated. That note is the brief.

## When is a logo cleanup enough?

A cleanup is enough when the name is staying, the offer is the same, and the main failures are technical or cosmetic: poor files, inconsistent colour, an outdated effect, or a mark that is too detailed to embroider.

In that case, keep what people already know. Refine the letterforms, reduce the clutter, and build the small set of versions you actually use: one for dark backgrounds, one for light, one for a tight square, and one that still works in a single colour on a site sign or a hard hat.

Do not let a cleanup quietly become a rename. If someone starts adding a new slogan, a second icon and a different colour story “while we are in there,” you are now in a rebrand without having agreed to one.

A cleanup should still end with a short handoff: usable files, colour references, and notes a printer can follow. If the delivery is a single flattened image, you will be having this conversation again next spring.

## When do you actually need a rebrand?

You need a broader identity project when the business has changed, or when the current system cannot be repaired without confusing the people who already know you.

Good reasons:

* A merger, a new partner, or a name that no longer describes the work.
* A reputation problem tied to the old identity, not just a taste change.
* Growth into a different buyer group, for example from residential-only in Edmonton to industrial work across Alberta, if the current look and language cannot stretch that far.
* An identity so inconsistent that every new application starts from scratch.

Bad reasons: a competitor launched a nicer Instagram grid, a relative said the logo looks dated, or someone promised that a new icon would make the phone ring. A new mark cannot carry a weak offer, and it will not fix a website that never names the service.

If the name is changing, plan the practical leftovers: domain, email, vehicle lettering, uniforms, listings and the website. A beautiful launch file is a problem if the old name is still on the box of the truck six months later.

## What should a usable system include for a mobile crew?

Keep it small and build for the surfaces you already have.

Most Alberta trades need:

1. A name and a short descriptor for when the name alone is unclear.
2. A logo system that works large on a vehicle and small on a profile photo.
3. Colour and type rules that a supplier can follow.
4. A voice that sounds like the people who answer the phone.
5. Artwork or guidance for the next three applications, not the next thirty.

For a mobile crew, those applications are usually the website, business cards or leave-behinds, and the vehicle. Signage and uniforms follow when they are real needs. This is not a substitute for the [storefront sign guide](/blog/storefront-sign-design-edmonton/) or the [vehicle wrap guide](/blog/ai-vehicle-wrap-design-edmonton/). Those are production problems. The brand system should make those later jobs easier, not pretend to be the installed sign or the printed wrap.

Look at [selected work](/work/) for how identity shows up on a live site and on cards. The test is consistency you can feel at a glance, not a decorative pattern applied to everything.

## A brief you can send before anyone starts drawing

Fill this in before you ask for options:

> We are [company], and we sell [service] to [buyer] in [service area]. The current identity is [staying / being refined / being replaced] because [specific problem]. These surfaces matter first: [website, cards, van, uniforms]. We must keep [name, colour, icon, or nothing]. We cannot use [anything that no longer tells the truth]. Approver: [the actual decision-maker].

Bring the files you have, even the bad ones, and photographs of the truck and the current site. A designer who has never seen the van will design a mark that only works in a presentation.

KR8TIV will often recommend the smaller job when that is the honest one. A full rebrand is more work, and it should have a reason that survives contact with a jobsite.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does branding cost for an Alberta trades business?

It depends on the system you need, not on a single logo file. KR8TIV’s published identity work starts from $500 CAD, with deliverables agreed in the scope. A full system that covers the website, cards and vehicle artwork will sit higher than a cleanup of an existing mark.

### Can I keep my current logo and still improve the brand?

Often yes. If the name still works and the mark is recognisable, a refinement of colour, typography and file quality may be enough. A full rebrand is for when the identity is confusing, outdated in a way that costs work, or no longer matches what you sell.

### Does a rebrand include the website and business cards?

It can. The identity should be designed to live on those surfaces, but each piece is listed in the scope. Agree what will be produced now, and keep the system ready for the next application.

### Do Edmonton trades really need a brand guide?

You need rules you can hand to a sign shop, a printer or a website designer without the mark getting redrawn every time. That can be a short, usable guide rather than a 40-page book.
