# How to build a marketing campaign that brings in customers in Edmonton

A useful marketing campaign connects one customer problem to one clear offer, then measures whether the resulting enquiries become profitable work. Start with the job you want to sell, build a page that answers the buyer’s questions, and choose a channel that reaches people who need that job done.

At KR8TIV · kr8tiv.io, the creative brief starts with the business outcome. The studio designs immersive visual experiences and helps businesses set up their brands and find clients. A sharp video matters. So does a memorable website. But neither can decide which customers you can serve profitably. Make that decision before you commission the creative.

This guide builds a campaign for a fictional Edmonton service business. Every number is an illustration, not a client result or a forecast. Use the worksheet to build your own version.

## What does a campaign need before you spend money?

You need an offer, a defined service area, a way to respond to enquiries, and a measurement plan. Without those, extra traffic can create extra admin instead of customers.

Write this sentence first:

> We want [number] qualified enquiries for [specific service] from [specific buyer] in [service area] by [review date], at an acquisition cost our margin supports.

Our example business offers commercial floor-coating assessments in Edmonton. It can serve nearby industrial properties, but long travel makes small jobs harder to justify. Its campaign therefore targets facility decision-makers inside its real operating area, rather than everyone in Alberta.

KR8TIV’s [project process](https://kr8tiv.io/process) explains how creative work moves from the brief to delivery. For your own campaign, define a qualified enquiry before launch. For this example, it means a commercial property, a suitable floor, a workable location, and a person able to discuss the project. Someone requesting a service the business does not provide is an enquiry, but not a qualified one.

## How do you choose an offer people actually want?

Choose the next useful step in the buyer’s decision, not a discount by default. The offer must explain what happens, what the buyer receives, and what commitment is involved.

A vague offer says, “Get quality service.” A useful version says, “Request a floor-coating assessment. Send your approximate floor area, current surface, location, and intended use so we can discuss suitable next steps.”

Only promise a free assessment, a fixed turnaround, or a warranty when you can honour it. If a site visit has a fee, explain that before booking. Buyers appreciate knowing the rules.

Interview three recent customers, with permission, or review your own enquiry notes. Ask what triggered their search, what they worried about, and what they needed before agreeing to a quote. Keep their names and private details out of public copy unless they approve their use.

Turn the answers into a short brief:

* The triggering problem in the customer’s own language.
* The service that addresses that problem.
* The practical limits, including location and availability.
* The evidence needed to feel comfortable enquiring.
* The next step and what happens after it.

## How much can you afford to acquire a customer?

Work backwards from contribution margin, not revenue. [Contribution margin](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/ca/resources/midsize-business/contribution-margin/) is sales revenue minus variable costs—the costs that change with the work delivered. It is not net profit: fixed costs still have to be covered.

Suppose a fictional job brings in CAD $2,000 and costs CAD $1,200 to deliver. That leaves CAD $800 before overhead and profit. Spending CAD $700 to acquire that customer would leave little room for either.

For an initial planning exercise, suppose the owner sets a maximum acquisition cost of CAD $200 after considering those needs. If one in four qualified enquiries becomes a customer, the implied maximum cost per qualified enquiry is CAD $50:

> CAD $200 acquisition allowance × 25% close rate = CAD $50 per qualified enquiry.

Those inputs are assumptions until the business has reliable data. Write them down anyway. A visible assumption is easier to correct than an unspoken hope.

Include creative production, management, and other campaign costs when judging total acquisition cost. Advertising platforms usually report media performance, not the full cost of getting a customer.

## What should the landing page say?

The landing page should repeat the offer, establish fit, address the main concern, and make the next step clear. Someone who clicks an Edmonton floor-coating ad should not have to decode a general homepage.

Draft the page in this order:

1. Name the service and the area you actually serve.
2. Explain the problem you handle and who the service suits.
3. Show real work or a clearly labelled demonstration.
4. Explain the assessment, quotation, and scheduling process.
5. Answer questions about preparation, disruption, and suitability.
6. Give one main action, with a working phone option if appropriate.

Use original photographs with permission. Caption them with useful context, such as the surface and preparation method. A photograph does not prove an outcome you have not measured.

Keep the enquiry form proportionate. Ask for location, project type, contact details, and the detail required to assess fit. Explain why you need photographs if you request them. Test the confirmation message and make sure the enquiry reaches the person answering it.

For Edmonton businesses, practical details can carry more weight than another slogan. Explain access requirements, weather-dependent work, and service boundaries where relevant. Avoid claiming winter affects a service when it does not.

## Which channel should you choose first?

Choose the channel that matches how the buyer reaches a decision. Search ads can reach existing demand. Social creative can demonstrate an unfamiliar service. Useful articles can answer questions before a buyer is ready to enquire.

For our example, a narrowly focused search campaign might test demand for commercial floor-coating services. A short video could show the assessment process for people comparing options. A blog could explain how to prepare a facility for a quote.

Do not launch every channel at once with a small budget. Start where you have a clear hypothesis and enough resources to learn something. If people already search for your exact service, test that demand before paying to explain the category to a broad audience.

For a service that relies on referrals and a long buying cycle, direct relationships may matter more than ads. The campaign can support those conversations with a strong page and a useful project guide.

## How do you make the creative consistent without making it repetitive?

Keep the offer and visual identity consistent while testing one meaningful difference. That lets you learn why a version works.

Create two versions of the same campaign. One could lead with reduced disruption during an assessment. The other could lead with understanding whether the existing surface is suitable. Only use claims the service can support.

Give each version the same destination, audience, and basic offer during the comparison. If you change the video, audience, page, and price together, you cannot tell which change affected results.

A useful video shows the actual process with readable captions and permission to film. A still image can show the same idea. Save the elaborate production for the question that needs it. Clear footage of a useful detail can do more teaching than an impressive opening animation.

## What should you measure after launch?

Measure qualified enquiries, response time, quotes, sales, and contribution after campaign costs. Clicks and views help diagnose delivery, but they do not tell you whether the work is profitable.

Maintain a small enquiry log. Record date, source when known, service requested, qualified status, next action, quote value, and final outcome. Collect only what you need and restrict access to customer details.

Use tagged campaign links, a tested form-success event, and a source question such as “How did you hear about us?” None provides perfect attribution. A prospect may watch a video, search your name later, and then call from a saved number.

Review the log alongside analytics. Plenty of traffic with no enquiries suggests an offer, fit, page, or tracking problem. Qualified enquiries with few quotes may point to follow-up or capacity. Plenty of quotes with few sales needs a different conversation from low click-through rate.

## What does a sensible first month look like?

Use the first month to validate the offer and the enquiry process, not to declare permanent success or failure from a handful of clicks.

In week one, write the brief, check the economics, and gather approved material. In week two, build the page and test the complete enquiry path on a phone. Launch only after someone receives a test lead and knows how to handle it.

During the next two weeks, check spend and delivery regularly. Review actual enquiries with the owner. Remove clearly irrelevant targeting and repair broken tracking promptly. Keep a change log so you know what happened when.

At the review date, decide whether to continue, revise, or stop. A small sample may be inconclusive. Do not increase spending just to make a campaign feel active.

## Use a decision sheet, not just a campaign report

Give the owner and the person answering enquiries the same short review sheet. A dashboard screenshot alone rarely captures whether the work suits the business.

| Record | What to enter |
| --- | --- |
| Hypothesis | The service, buyer and next step being tested |
| Authorised costs | Media, setup, creative and management, kept separate |
| Enquiries received | Actual enquiries, with spam and test submissions identified |
| Qualification | The agreed criteria, applied consistently |
| Sales progress | Follow-up, quotes and won work without counting a quote as revenue |
| Unknowns | Missing attribution, unresolved quotes and incomplete tracking |
| Decision | Continue, change one thing, pause or stop—and why |

For the fictional floor-coating campaign, ten form submissions might include four unsuitable residential requests. Keep those four visible in the review. Hiding them makes the acquisition cost appear better and removes evidence that the message or targeting needs work.

Record open opportunities separately from lost ones. A commercial buyer still discussing access is not automatically a failed lead because the reporting month ended. Conversely, an unanswered enquiry is not evidence that the offer worked.

When the creative changes, keep the previous files and log the change date. The [brand system](https://kr8tiv.io/services/branding/) should stay recognisable while you test the message; you do not need a new identity for every experiment.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I market a small business in Edmonton on a limited budget?

Start with one profitable service, one realistic service area, and a page that helps people enquire. Use customer questions to choose content and test one suitable acquisition channel before adding more.

### Should I advertise across Alberta right away?

Only if you can serve that area profitably. Travel, delivery, scheduling, and follow-up capacity matter more than the size of the audience.

### How long does a marketing campaign take to work?

Delivery and early enquiries can appear before there is enough evidence about profitable sales. Set a review period around your buying cycle and budget. No fixed timeline guarantees results.

### Can video help a trades marketing campaign?

Yes, when it answers a buying question. Show preparation, access, process, or a finished detail with permission. Use captions and a clear next step rather than relying on entertainment alone.
