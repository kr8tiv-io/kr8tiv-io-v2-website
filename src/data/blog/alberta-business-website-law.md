# What the law expects from an Alberta small business website in 2026

If your website has a contact form, a newsletter sign-up or prices, the law already applies to it. In Alberta, the main rules are the provincial Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) for personal information, the federal anti-spam law (CASL) for commercial emails and texts, and the Competition Act for advertised prices and reviews. Accessibility is a softer area, but not an empty one.

This is not legal advice. I design and build websites. I am not a lawyer. What I can do is point to the sections that matter, explain them in plain language, and show what they look like on a real small business site. If your business handles health, financial or children's information, or sends a lot of marketing, get a lawyer to review your setup. I checked every source below on 19 November 2026.

## Privacy: Alberta's PIPA

Most Alberta businesses are covered by the [Personal Information Protection Act](https://kings-printer.alberta.ca/serve_pdf.cfm?file=P06P5.pdf&type=Acts), not the federal privacy law. The Province has a plain-language summary of [what organizations are responsible for](https://www.alberta.ca/organization-responsibilities-for-protecting-personal-information). On a website, it comes up every time someone fills in a form.

The sections that matter most for a small business site:

**Section 6. Have policies, and share them.** You must develop and follow reasonable policies and practices for personal information, and make written information about them available on request. If you use a service provider outside Canada to collect or store information, your policies have to say which countries and for what purposes.

That last part catches most small sites. Website builders, form tools, email platforms and analytics are very often hosted outside Canada. If your contact form sends to a US-hosted service, you are using a service provider outside Canada.

**Section 7. Consent.** You generally need consent to collect, use or disclose someone's personal information.

**Section 13. Tell people why, before you collect.** Before or at the time you collect information from someone, you must tell them the purpose, and the name or title of a person who can answer their questions about it.

**Section 13.1. Tell people about foreign service providers.** If a service provider outside Canada collects the information for you, you must tell people how to get written information about your practices on that, and who can answer their questions.

**Section 34. Keep it secure.** Make reasonable security arrangements against unauthorized access and similar risks.

**Section 34.1. Report serious breaches.** If personal information is lost or accessed without authorization and a reasonable person would consider there is a real risk of significant harm, you must notify the Information and Privacy Commissioner without unreasonable delay.

**Section 35. Do not keep it forever.** Keep personal information only as long as you reasonably need it, then destroy it or make it non-identifying.

The penalties for an offence under [section 59](https://kings-printer.alberta.ca/serve_pdf.cfm?file=P06P5.pdf&type=Acts) go up to $10,000 for an individual and $100,000 for a business.

### What that looks like on a contact form

A short line under the form usually does most of the work:

> We use your details only to reply to your enquiry and, if you go ahead, to deliver the job. Our form and email are hosted by [provider] in [country]. Questions about your information? Contact [name or title] at [email]. Read our privacy page.

Then a privacy page that explains what you collect, why, where it is stored, how long you keep it, and how people can ask to see or correct it. The Office of the Information and Privacy Commissioner publishes [ten steps to implement PIPA](https://oipc.ab.ca/resource/pipa-implementation/) that work well as a checklist.

### Is PIPA changing?

Possibly. Alberta's [PIPA engagement page](https://www.alberta.ca/personal-information-protection-act-engagement) says a legislative committee finished a review in February 2025 with 12 recommendations, a public survey ran from 2 to 17 February 2026, and the Province was meeting the Commissioner's office in spring 2026. When I checked, the results were "under review" and no amendment bill had been introduced. If one passes, I will update this page.

## Email and texts: CASL

Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation covers commercial electronic messages, which the Act [defines](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/section-1.html) as messages where one of the purposes is to encourage participation in a commercial activity. Newsletters, promotions and "we have a sale" texts are all covered.

**Consent.** [Section 6](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/section-6.html) requires express or implied consent. Under [section 10](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/section-10.html), a purchase gives implied consent for two years, and an inquiry for six months. To keep emailing after that, you need express consent. The federal [CASL consent guide](https://fightspam-combattrelepourriel.ised-isde.canada.ca/site/canada-anti-spam-legislation/en/getting-consent-send-email) says you should be ready to prove consent if asked, so keep a record.

**On a website, that means:**

* A newsletter box that is not pre-ticked, with a plain description of what people will get.
* No quietly adding every contact form enquiry to your newsletter list.
* Storing when and how each person signed up.

**Every message must** identify your business, give contact details that stay valid for at least 60 days, and include an unsubscribe option. [Section 11](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/section-11.html) gives you 10 business days to act on an unsubscribe.

The maximum administrative penalties in [section 20](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/E-1.6/section-20.html) are $1 million per violation for an individual and $10 million for a business. If you ask customers for Google reviews by email or text, read [how to get reviews legally in Canada](/blog/google-reviews-canada-rules/).

## Prices: drip pricing

If you show prices on your site, the Competition Bureau's guidance on [drip pricing](https://competition-bureau.canada.ca/en/deceptive-marketing-practices/drip-pricing) applies. Its rule is simple. Do not advertise a price that cannot be paid because of mandatory fixed fees added later. The only charges you can add on top are ones imposed by government on the buyer, such as GST. The Bureau also warns that a variable mandatory fee can still raise concerns.

So "Cleaning from $149" with a mandatory $25 "booking fee" at checkout is a problem. Fold the fee into the advertised price, or drop it. The Bureau's page links to its case against Cineplex over online ticket fees, which it won.

"From" prices are fine when the lowest price is genuinely available.

## Reviews and testimonials

The Competition Act also covers reviews. The Competition Bureau has warned that [employee reviews without a disclosed connection](https://www.canada.ca/en/competition-bureau/news/2024/01/online-reviews-posted-by-employees-businesses-could-be-liable.html) can make the business liable, and that this applies to testimonials on social media too. On your own site, use real testimonials from real customers, with permission. Do not edit them to say something the customer did not.

## Accessibility

This is where Alberta differs from Ontario.

* **No provincial accessibility act for businesses.** Bill 206, the Accessible Alberta Act, was introduced as a private member's bill in March 2026 and [defeated at second reading on 23 March 2026](https://www.assembly.ab.ca/LAO/Bills/bsr31-2.pdf).
* **The federal [Accessible Canada Act](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/accessible-canada.html)** applies to federally regulated sectors, such as banking, telecommunications and interprovincial transport. Most Edmonton shops and trades are not covered.
* **The Alberta Human Rights Act still applies.** It bans discrimination in [goods and services customarily available to the public](https://albertahumanrights.ab.ca/issues-with-services/goods-and-services/), and the Alberta Human Rights Commission explains a [duty to accommodate](https://albertahumanrights.ab.ca/issues-with-services/duty-to-accommodate-in-goods-and-services/) up to the point of undue hardship. I am not aware of an Alberta ruling about a small business website, but the principle is there.

My advice is practical rather than legal. Build to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines anyway: readable contrast, real text instead of text in images, labelled form fields, alt text on images, and a site that works with a keyboard. It costs little when it is done at the start, and it helps every customer on a phone in bright sunlight, not only people with disabilities.

## A one-page checklist

* A privacy page that covers purpose, foreign service providers, retention, and a named contact.
* A short notice under every form.
* Newsletter sign-up with an unticked box, and records of consent.
* Every marketing email and text: business name, contact details, working unsubscribe.
* Advertised prices that include every mandatory fixed fee.
* Real, permitted testimonials. No staff reviews without disclosure.
* Accessible design: contrast, alt text, labels, keyboard use.
* Business name, City licence and contact details easy to find. See [starting a business in Edmonton](/blog/starting-a-business-edmonton/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does an Alberta small business website need a privacy policy?

If the site collects personal information, such as names, emails or phone numbers through a form, Alberta's Personal Information Protection Act applies. It requires reasonable policies and practices, a notice of purpose and a contact person at the time of collection, and written information about your practices on request. A clear privacy page is the practical way to do that.

### Do I need consent to add website enquiries to my newsletter?

Under CASL, you need consent to send commercial emails. An enquiry gives implied consent for six months, and a purchase for two years. To keep sending after that, you need express consent, such as an unticked opt-in box. Every message needs your business details and a working unsubscribe link.

### Is website accessibility the law in Alberta?

Alberta does not have an accessibility act for private businesses. Bill 206, the proposed Accessible Alberta Act, was defeated in March 2026. The federal Accessible Canada Act covers federally regulated sectors. The Alberta Human Rights Act still bans discrimination in services offered to the public.

### Can I advertise a price on my website and add fees at checkout?

Not mandatory fixed fees. The Competition Bureau says advertising a price that cannot be paid because of added mandatory fees, known as drip pricing, breaks the law. Only charges imposed by government, such as GST, can be added on top.
