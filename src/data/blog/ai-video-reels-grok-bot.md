# How we make AI video reels with Grok Bot, step by step

We make a short AI reel by handing Grok Bot the pipeline and keeping the taste with a person. The bot pulls the beats out of the brief, prompts Grok Imagine, writes the compositor, and checks the frames, the kicks and the loudness. I approve the joke, the stills and the post. Nothing goes up until that yes.

A short reel is a 30 second argument with someone who is already scrolling. You get about 2.5 seconds of setup to earn the title, and the picture has to work with the sound off. The model will happily animate a grey website, a dinner plate or a monster. It will not know which one is the joke. You still have to decide.

The method below is the one we locked on two of our own cuts, finished on 26 and 27 November 2026, rejects included. The 26 November reel is the house standard. The 27 November cut is the second film on the same pipeline.

## What is Grok Bot doing on the reel?

Grok Bot is the worker on the pipeline. xAI's guide, checked 27 November 2026, calls it an AI teammate with a computer. It works in connected apps and on a cloud computer, so the job continues after the laptop is shut. It can use plugins and other connected tools, and it can hand the coding to a Cursor cloud agent.

On our reels we give it six jobs.

1. Pull the beats out of the raw brief, and keep the lines that are a claim, a joke or a feeling.
2. Open a primary source for any number that will go on screen, and drop the number if that source cannot be opened.
3. Prompt Grok Imagine with the references, the action and a ban on text.
4. Write and run the compositor: type, wordmark, beat grid, speed changes and the end card, laid on top of the approved picture.
5. Check frame strips, loudness and kicks.
6. Log the script, the licence, the rejects and the decisions the same day.

## What does Grok Imagine actually make?

Imagine does three jobs on these reels. Spelling is a fourth job, and it belongs to a person.

**Stills.** A prompt, the reference images, and a vertical 9:16 frame. Lock the character, the expression, the prop, and a dark patch where the type will sit later.

**Image to video.** The approved still is the first frame, and the model moves it. xAI's docs, checked 27 November 2026, say image-to-video on `grok-imagine-video-1.5` is native 1080p, and a clip can run from 1 second up to 15 seconds. We generate 6 second takes.

**First and last frame.** Pin both stills and the model fills the move between them. That is how a character grows across one shot. xAI treats that pin as reference-to-video, and reference mode tops out at 720p. Our first morph came back at 480p. We asked again at 720p and scaled that file up. The scaled file is the one in the reel, a step below the 1080p clips around it.

**Words, logo, price.** Type, the wordmark, a price, a phone number and the spelling of a name go on in code, from the real logo file, after the picture is approved. Generated letters warp. A drawn logo is not your logo.

## How do we keep the same character in every shot?

Pass a clean reference, with no type in it, into every later still. A prompt that only says "a blue monster" will invent a cousin, and cousins fall apart over 30 seconds. The 26 November reel carried a prior frame plus a hero sheet, and it named the character the same way every time: "the same large fluffy blue monster with purple highlights and curved brown horns" and "the same small brass vintage robot with glowing yellow eyes". The 27 November monster is a different creature, teal, with a giant cyan-violet brain, plus that same robot. One film, one creature.

A still prompt, shortened, with the rude title left out:

The same small brass robot with glowing yellow eyes, both arms up. Cinematic dark background, heavy vignette, characters low in the frame. No text, no logos, no letters anywhere.

Characters stay low so the top third is dark enough for type. Screens stay wordless: grey rectangles for a template, magenta, orange and cyan for a custom site. Name the prop narrowly. ## How does a still become a clip?

The video prompt is shorter, and it is stricter about speed.

> Real-time normal speed, no slow motion. The monster tears the page in fast cartoon timing. The robot throws both arms up. One gentle push-in. No text appears.

Review a strip of about nine frames. Reject surprise letters, a warped face, an extra limb, or slow motion that arrived uninvited. On a shot that carries the title, tell the model "Camera completely locked, no zoom, no push-in." The 27 November open pushed in and parked the brain under the hook, so the title had nowhere to sit. The reshoot stayed locked. A crash zoom, when we want one, is built in the compositor, and it lands on a kick.

## How do we cut motion so the reel does not feel like one trick?

The look stays put: dark ground, a heavy vignette, hard cuts, gold and cyan, the official wordmark. What changes is how each shot enters. The same entrance on every shot gives the trick away by the third one.

The rotation is a crash zoom, a one-hit glitch, type that slams onto the kick, a tear into the next picture, a whip on the type only, a split screen, a zoom from inside the tablet out to the dinner, and a little parallax. Whipping the whole frame showed black edges, so the whip stays on the words. Speed runs from fast to normal only. The shred plays at 1.6 times and eases back. Titles sit on the real drum hits. The first grid on the 32 second reel was a quarter beat late, and the corrected times are in the table at the end.

The wordmark sits up top. Setups are a near-white, punchlines an italic gold. Keep the bottom clear for the platform buttons. ## What about music, loudness and voices?

Licensed tracks only. Both reels use Mixkit hip-hop by Arulo: "Young Trizzy 02" and "Thunder". The licence is Mixkit's Stock Music Free License. I opened the licence index and the modal on 27 November 2026.

For a business video, that licence allows commercial and non-commercial projects, including social posts, online ads, YouTube, podcasts and educational use. It does not allow CDs or DVDs, television or radio, video games, a music-only remix, claiming the track, or registering it with a rights service. We keep a licence note with every file: title, artist, URL, download day and a file hash.

> If you cannot name the licence, you have a takedown waiting for a quiet Tuesday.

Pick the track first. The house range is about 80 to 100 beats per minute: a quiet intro, a drop for the title, a break for the end card. "Young Trizzy 02" is cut so the drop lands on the title. "Thunder" sits near 95 beats a minute, and its drop hits the title at 3.00 seconds. Both files fade across the last 1.3 seconds. There are no separate sound effects.

LUFS is how loud the whole file feels. True peak is the spike, and a spike over 0 crackles on a phone. These reels aim near -14 LUFS, with the limiter set for a true peak of -1.5 dBTP. The 32 second reel met the loudness aim and ran hotter than the peak target. The 45 second music file peaked above 0, and it took two limiter stages to bring the master under that target. Measure the file you keep.

These two reels do not speak. When one does, the Evolve Bigfoot shorts set the rules. One speaker per clip, and the listener's mouth stays closed, or the shot feels haunted. The same voice sentence goes in every prompt. Generate about 6 seconds from a clean still, trim it, put the speech in the first half second, and watch the mouth. Duck any music under the line.

## What gets checked before anything posts?

The cut has to survive a pass that is allowed to fail it. We watch a contact sheet and a full-size frame at every cut: type off the face, lines apart, hits landing with the drums, no stray letters, the official wordmark, loudness measured on the finished file. A 16:9 gets its own pass when one exists. Anything we leave in is written down: a dark edge on a whip, an upscaled morph, a pointer gone for a few frames. An unlisted flaw is a surprise on a phone.

Then someone who did not make the cut reads it against the approved script. A self-check has passed work that the second look failed. Nothing posts without the owner's yes. On our reels that person is me. On a client reel it is the client.

## When is an AI reel the wrong tool?

An AI reel earns its place when the picture is an argument: a feeling, a joke, a way of showing the offer. It is the wrong tool when the viewer is meant to believe the screen is literally true.

* **Real staff.** Film the person who will show up. A horned monster can hold a point of view. It cannot be your estimator, your chef or your crew lead.
* **A real testimonial.** Use the customer's face, their words and their permission. A stranger built to say a sentence a customer once said is still a fiction.
* **Exact product appearance.** Photograph the object when the colour, the label, the fit or the part is what you sell. If the buyer will compare the picture with the thing in their hand, the picture has to be the thing.
* **Safety-critical work.** Fall protection, guards, a shored trench, a tied-off roof. If a wrong picture could teach a wrong habit, film the real site and the real gear.
* **Anything else that must be literally true.** A price, a phone number, a before-and-after of a finished job, a face, a licence plate, a certificate. Real type from a real file, or a camera.

What you film instead is a straight edit of footage you already have, or a half day in the shop, on the truck, or with the person who does the work. It costs a project, and it is the right answer when the picture has to be true.

## Try it on your own business this week

You can test one offer this week, before you hire anyone. Give it an afternoon. Stop when you have one clip you would let a stranger watch with the sound off.

1. Write the offer in one line, in the customer's words: the thing they can buy, and why it is the one they want. A paragraph means you are writing a page.
2. Split that line into three beats, the problem, the turn and the proof, and assign each beat one picture you can see with the sound off.
3. Make one reference still. Vertical, no words in the frame, and a dark area up top for type later. Later clips inherit this still.
4. Approve the still before you spend a clip. Fix a warped face, an extra finger, a stray logo, or letters hiding in a sign while the fix is still cheap.
5. Generate one 6 second clip from that still: normal speed, one action, no text. If a limb wanders or the motion goes slow, reject it and generate another.
6. Add the words and your real logo in an editor you already know, on top of the picture. The picture does not spell, and a drawn logo is not your logo.
7. Lay in licensed music. Write the title, the artist, the URL and the download day beside the file. If you cannot name the licence, pick another track.
8. Watch it on a phone with the sound off. If the offer is unclear, change the type or the picture, then ask a second person who did not make the cut to say yes. Your own yes does not count.

One clip that survives with the sound off is a method. A clip that only works while you narrate it is not finished.
