# How we make AI video reels with Grok Bot

A short reel is a 30 second argument. The joke lands in about 2.5 seconds, the picture has to work with the sound off, and a person still has to say the cut is true.

This is how KR8TIV does AI video production Edmonton work. If you searched how to make reels with AI, this is the studio cut, not a tool list. Grok Bot plans the beats, prompts the pictures, writes the compositing code and checks its own work. Grok Imagine makes the stills and the clips. I write the brief and keep the taste. Nothing posts until I say yes. The two reels below shipped on 26 and 27 November 2026, rejects included.

You do not need a film crew for this kind of video. You do need a point of view. The model will animate a grey website, a dinner plate and a monster. It will not know which one is the joke.

## What is Grok Bot doing in our pipeline?

Grok Bot is not a caption button. xAI's guide, checked 27 November 2026, calls it an AI teammate with a computer: connected apps, a cloud computer that keeps working with the laptop shut, plugins and custom MCP servers, and the option to hand coding to a Cursor cloud agent.

On a reel it does six jobs. It pulls beats from the raw brief and keeps the lines that are a claim, a joke or a feeling. It opens a primary source for any number that will go on screen, and drops the number if the source cannot be opened. It prompts Grok Imagine with the references, the action and the ban on text. It writes and runs the compositor in Python, Pillow, numpy and ffmpeg: type, wordmark, beat grid, speed ramps, end card. It checks frame strips, loudness and kicks. ## What does a Grok Imagine video actually include?

Grok Imagine does three jobs in this pipeline, and it does not do a fourth.

**Stills.** Prompt plus reference images, vertical 9:16. Lock the character, the expression, the prop and the empty space for type.

**Image to video.** The still is the first frame. xAI's docs, checked 27 November 2026, say image-to-video on `grok-imagine-video-1.5` is native 1080p and a clip can run up to 15 seconds. We generate 6 second takes. The November files measured 1088 by 1920, 24 frames a second, 145 frames.

**First and last frame.** Pin both stills and the model fills the move, which is how a character grows without a cut. That pin is reference-to-video, and reference mode tops out at 720p. Our first morph came back at 480p. We asked again at 720p and scaled up. Written down as a compromise.

**Not its job.** Type, the wordmark, your price, your phone number and the spelling of your name go on in code, from the real logo file, after the picture is approved.

## How do we keep the same character in every shot?

Pass a clean, type-free reference into every later still. "A blue monster" will invent a cousin. The template reel used a prior frame plus a hero sheet, and named "the same large fluffy blue monster with purple highlights and curved brown horns" and "the same small brass vintage robot with glowing yellow eyes" every time. The AI reel's monster is teal, with a giant cyan-violet brain, plus that same robot. Do not mix the two monsters.

A still prompt, shortened and with the rude title removed:

> Pixar-quality 3D animated film still, vertical 9:16. The same large fluffy blue monster with purple highlights and curved brown horns from the reference images, gleeful, ripping a blank grey page into confetti. The same small brass robot with glowing yellow eyes, both arms up. Cinematic dark background, heavy vignette, characters low in the frame. No text, no logos, no letters anywhere.

Characters stay low so the top third is dark enough for type. Screens stay wordless: grey rectangles for a template, magenta, orange and cyan for a custom site. Name the prop. "Knife switch" once put a real knife in the robot's hand. The fix was the wooden handle of an old electrical switch, on the wall, no blade. Another still painted a car maker's emblem on the lab stone. The retake asked for plain walls. Check the still before you spend the clip.

## How does a still become a clip?

The video prompt is shorter, and stricter about speed.

> Real-time normal speed, no slow motion. The monster tears the page in fast cartoon timing. The robot throws both arms up. One gentle push-in. No text appears.

Review a strip of about nine frames. Reject surprise letters, a warped face, an extra limb, or slow motion. On a title shot add "Camera completely locked, no zoom, no push-in." Drift on the AI reel parked the brain under the hook. The reshoot stayed locked, and the compositor zoom stayed near 6 percent. A crash zoom, when we want one, is ours and it lands on a kick.

## How do we cut motion so the reel does not feel like one trick?

Dark ground, heavy vignette, hard cuts, gold and cyan, the official wordmark. The motion changes every shot. The menu: crash zoom, a one-hit glitch, kinetic type in about a tenth of a second, a tear that reveals the next picture rather than black, a whip on the type layer only (the whole frame showed black edges), split screen, a zoom from inside the tablet out to the dinner, and a little parallax.

Speed ramps go fast to normal only. Never slow motion. A ramp might play the first second at roughly double time, then sit at 1x. Titles sit on detected kicks under about 150 Hz, not on a beat tracker that was a quarter beat off. The AI reel's syncopated drums needed hard-coded hits at 3.01, 3.95, 4.59 and 5.06 seconds.

Wordmark up top, mono kickers, setups in white `#ececec`, punchlines in italic gold `#c1a65e`. Nothing important in the bottom, where the platform buttons sit. A big head, like the AI reel's brain, pushes copy to a lower third. Lines do not touch. The wordmark is the official file from `kr8tiv-logo-clean.svg`, never a redraw and never a heavier stand-in. The 16:9 is its own layout, picture in a feathered panel on the right, type on the left, not a pillarbox. The AI reel still has no native 16:9. That gap is written down.

Encode is H.264 High, slow preset, CRF 19, AAC 256 kbps at 48 kHz, so playback can start before the download finishes. Each shape of the 32 second reel took about 4 minutes.

## What about music, loudness and voices?

Licensed tracks only. Both reels use Mixkit hip-hop by Arulo: "Young Trizzy 02" and "Thunder", under the Mixkit Stock Music Free License. The licence index, opened 27 November 2026, still lists a free licence for stock music. Terms recorded with the files on 26 and 27 November allow commercial and non-commercial use, including social video, online ads, YouTube and podcasts, with no attribution. They do not allow TV or radio, CDs and DVDs, video games, a music-only remix, or registering the track with a rights service. Each build folder keeps the title, artist, URL, download day and a file hash. Pick the track before the timing: about 80 to 100 beats per minute, a quiet intro, a drop for the title, a break for the card. "Young Trizzy 02" starts at 11.14 seconds of the source so the open lasts 2.58 seconds and the drop kick (source 13.72 seconds) hits the title. "Thunder", about 95.7 beats per minute, starts at 17.21 seconds and the drop hits at 3.00 seconds. Fade the last 1.3 seconds. No separate sound effects.

LUFS is how loud the whole file feels. True peak is the spike, and a file over 0 clips when encoded. Marketing reels aim at about -14 LUFS, true peak under about -1.5 dBTP. The template reel landed at -14.0 LUFS and about -1.1 dBTP. The AI reel's Mixkit file peaked above 0 dBTP, and one loudness pass left the audio at -0.4 dBTP. A two-stage limiter brought the 45.00 second master to -14.8 LUFS and -2.1 dBTP.

> If you cannot name the licence, you do not have a soundtrack. You have a takedown waiting for a quiet Tuesday.

These marketing reels do not speak. When a film does, use the Evolve Bigfoot rules: one speaker per clip, listener's mouth closed, the same voice sentence every time, generate about 6 seconds from a clean still and trim, speech inside the first half second, watch the mouth, duck the music. Ogopogo used "Feel Good Funk Beat" by JorisVermeer under the Pixabay Content License. That log, 25 October 2026, records commercial use, no attribution, -16.0 LUFS and -1.3 dBTP. Beaver Tail used room tone only, at -16 LUFS, true peak target -1.5 dBTP.

A character kit is the plain folder of approved stills, wardrobe, voice sentence and the prop that must not change shape.

## What gets checked before anything posts?

Proof, fix, repeat. Then a contact sheet of both finals and a full-size frame at every cut: type off the face, lines apart, kicks within a frame of the slams, no generated text, the official wordmark, loudness on the finished file, and the 16:9 on its own. Write down a dark whip edge, an upscaled morph, a pointer gone for a few frames.

Then someone who did not just defend the cut reads it against the approved script. A self-check has passed a cut that the second look failed.

Nothing posts without the owner's yes. On our reels that is me. On a client reel it is the client.

### Mistakes worth avoiding

| Mistake | What we do instead |
|---|---|
| Text inside the generated frame | Composite type and the real wordmark in code |
| Slow motion, or a raging character | Fast-to-normal ramps, and an arc of feelings |
| The same move on every shot | A different entry every shot |
| Trusting the beat grid | Detect the real kicks and lock the slams to them |
| A push-in on a shot that carries type | Lock the camera. Keep zooms in the compositor |
| Skipping the still check | Approve the still, then spend the clip |
| Unnamed music | A licence note with title, artist, URL, date and hash |
| Hot audio | About -14 LUFS on these reels, about -16 with dialogue, true peak under about -1.5 dBTP |
| Posting because the render finished | A second review, then the owner's yes |
