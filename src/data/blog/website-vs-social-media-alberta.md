# Do I still need a website if my Alberta business is already on Instagram and Facebook?

Yes, if you want a place you own that still answers the hiring questions when the algorithm is in a mood. Instagram and Facebook are good at showing the work. They are a shaky place to hang the entire business.

This comes up constantly with Edmonton and Alberta trades. The feed is already full of finished decks, clean welds, and roofing under a prairie sky. Why pay for a website? Because the customer who is ready to hire you is often not in a scrolling mood. They want to know whether you do their kind of job, whether you come to their area, and how to ask for a quote without sending a direct message into a void.

KR8TIV builds [websites in Edmonton](/services/web-design-edmonton/) for businesses that already have decent social followings, and for businesses that barely post. The useful question is not “social or website.” It is which job each one should do.

## What are Instagram and Facebook actually good at?

They are good at proof and personality. A short clip of a crew wrapping a job in St. Albert tells a buyer more than a stock photo of a handshake. A regular posting habit keeps you visible to people who already know you, and to the friends they tag.

They are also where a lot of first impressions now happen. If your only public face is a neglected profile with a dead phone number, that is the impression. A sharp profile is worth maintaining even after a website exists.

What they are not good at is being a stable record of the business. Posts sink. Highlights get messy. Links break. The layout changes. A new rule can make it harder to be reached, or push people toward a paid boost before they can read your service area.

A fictional Red Deer painter might get compliments every time they post a before-and-after. That is not the same as a saved page a property manager can forward to a colleague in January, when the original post is three hundred tiles back.

## What does a website still need to own?

A website should own the facts a serious buyer has to check. At minimum:

* What you do, in plain language.
* Where you actually work, including travel limits.
* Evidence that looks like your work, with permission to use it.
* What happens after someone contacts you.
* A phone number and form that still work when a platform does not.

Search is part of this, but it is not the only part. People type “eavestrough repair near me” in Edmonton. They also tap a link on a truck, follow a Google listing, or get your name from a neighbour and want to confirm you are real. Social profiles can appear in those moments. They rarely answer the whole question as clearly as a page built for it.

You also own a website in a way you do not own a profile. You can move hosts, keep the domain, and still be findable if a network fades. That sounds abstract until the day you cannot edit a page, or the username you wanted is gone, or the only enquiry path is a message request buried under scam replies.

If you are weighing a full build against a single focused page, the [website economics guide](/blog/website-that-earns-its-keep-alberta/) walks through that decision. Some Alberta businesses only need one strong offer page to start.

## How should the website and the social profiles work together?

Give them the same name, the same visual identity, and the same contact route. Then let each surface do its own job.

On social, show the work and send people somewhere useful. A profile link should land on the service you are actually promoting, not a vague homepage that makes the visitor start over. If you posted a concrete coating job in Leduc, the link should explain that service and how to request a quote.

On the website, include current photographs and, if you like, a tasteful link back to the profile where you post daily work. Do not make a visitor create an account or open an app to see basic pricing context, service area, or a phone number.

A simple rule: if someone saw you on a phone in a jobsite lunchroom, could they find you later by name and still get the same story? If the Facebook page says Edmonton only, the website says all of Alberta, and the Instagram bio says “DM for quotes,” you do not have a system. You have three slightly different businesses.

## A quick test before you decide to skip a website

Ask someone who does not work with you to hire you from your public presence alone. They may use Instagram, Facebook, Google, or your name typed into a browser. Watch where they get stuck.

If they cannot confirm the service, the area, or the next step without sending a message and waiting, you do not have a hiring path. You have a highlight reel.

If they can do all of that from a page you control, and social is adding proof on top, you are in better shape. Keep posting. Just stop asking a feed to be your office.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a website if I already have Instagram and Facebook?

Usually yes, if customers need a stable place to confirm what you do, where you work, and how to contact you. Social posts are useful proof. They are a weak substitute for a page you own, can be found in search, and can update without a platform changing the rules.

### Can an Edmonton trades business get by with a Facebook page alone?

Some work will still come from people who already know you. A Facebook page does not give you a reliable search presence, a clean quote path you control, or a link you can put on a truck and still trust in five years.

### Should I stop posting if I build a website?

No. Keep posting the work. Use social to show the job, and send people to a website page that explains the service, the area, and the next step. The two should share a name, a look, and a contact route.
